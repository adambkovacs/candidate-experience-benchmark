Do models like Jev get it right when correctness is business-critical?

On 60 test reviews, Jev matched our provisional reference on all four decisions for 54. General models, from Gemma 4 26B up to Opus 5.5, matched 58 or 59, for roughly 4 to 40 times the estimated cost of a Jev pass. Most of Jev's 6 misses sat on reviews a person would pause on. Two were plain errors, and one of those was a harassment report it marked as needing no follow-up.

If the decision matters, buy a rule that hands the hard reviews to a person.

The test

Every review gets four decisions.

The 60 reviews are short, fictional comments from job candidates. A model answers four questions about each one. A review counts as matched only when all four answers agree with the reference.

  1. How was the experience?Positive, negative, mixed, neutral, or can't tell.
  2. Does someone need to follow up?Yes, no, or can't tell.
  3. Does it report a serious concern?Harassment, discrimination or a privacy breach, for example. A bad experience alone does not count.
  4. Could it work as a testimonial?Yes, no, or can't tell.

01Three findings

Finding 1

The gap is five reviews.

Over three fresh passes, Jev matched 54, 53 and 52 of 60. Opus 5.5 at high effort matched 59, 58 and 58. Sonnet 5.5 at xhigh matched 58 in all nine prompt and pass cells.

A Jev pass cost $0.00589092, a known provider charge. An Opus pass is an API-equivalent estimate of $0.222052, not a bill.

In its direct P0 run, Jev matched all 25 reviews the reference flags as a serious concern. Twenty-five synthetic examples cannot show real-world sensitivity, and it still answered no to "does someone need to follow up?" on this one, where the reference says yes:

Nice staff. After the trial shift the manager kept asking me out even after I said no twice. I've asked the recruiter to stop him contacting me.

The reviews that split the models are the ones a person pauses on.

None of the seven decision models matched the reference on this review: 0 of 7. Among general-model configurations with valid answers, 89 of 113 did.

Finding 3

Neither confidence nor a longer prompt works as a gate.

A confidence score that tracks its hit rate

On these 60 reviews, Jev's confidence tracked how often it was right: a pooled calibration gap of 0.011, against 0.358 for Clef Flash. It was still 0.96 confident on a wrong testimonial answer. Its ten least-confident reviews include 7 of the 10 hardest, so the score flags hard reviews, though not on the fields you act on.

A longer prompt

Moving from classifier instructions (P1) to step-by-step decision rules (P2) raised the score for 4 of 39 setups, left 14 unchanged and lowered 21.

Calibration figures are descriptive and look back at the same reviews. Jev never abstained, and no threshold was chosen on held-out data.

Second-pass analysis

Recomputed findings from the saved answers

The talk's analysis scripts reran over the saved predictions for the same 60 fictional reviews and made no new model calls. Each number names its place in the data file, which also lists the SHA-256 of every source file it was built from.

This section draws its cards from the data file with JavaScript; the file holds every number and its source.

Limits. The numbers describe saved runs; they rank no model and make no claim about cause. The reference is provisional, and three of its labels are disputed. A missing cost stays unknown and never counts as zero, and provider charges stay separate from estimates. The section makes no speed claims. Every finding looks back at the same 60 development reviews, and repeated runs of them are not independent samples.

04Limits

Limits

What this test does not show.

  • It is not a leaderboard. Runs differ in route, interface, batch size, effort and prompt, so a ranking would compare unlike things.
  • Nothing here is causal. These are saved runs, compared after the fact.
  • It is not real-world accuracy. The 60 reviews are synthetic, written by AI and enriched with concerns.
  • The reference is provisional. A person checked every label on 2 October 2026. Three labels remain disputed: DEV-006, DEV-013 and DEV-030.
  • A missing cost means unknown. Known charges, observed charges and API-equivalent estimates are labelled and kept apart.
  • There are no speed claims. Recorded request times include the network, batching and the operator.
  • The 1,004 saved run entries reuse the same 60 reviews. They are repeats and variants of one small test.
  • Confidence thresholds here are hindsight. No threshold was chosen on held-out data, and Jev never abstained.

For your team

Questions to ask before an AI pilot

Read the latest analysis update →

Our fictional examples show what to investigate. They cannot tell you which system to deploy.

For the team using the answers

Choose real examples your team understands. Agree on what each label means and who should act on it. Review missed follow-ups and sensitive comments with a person.

For leaders approving a pilot

Ask who checked the expected answers, whether repeat tests changed the result, and what a mistake would cost the team. A high score on fictional examples is an early test, not deployment approval.

For people building the workflow

Record the exact model, hosting service, settings and inputs. Keep failed requests in the results. Test new instructions on the same examples, then repeat the test.
