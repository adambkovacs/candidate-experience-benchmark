Jev matched 54 of 60 reviews. The big general models matched 58 or 59.

Jev is TypeSafe's decision model. You give it text and a typed question, and it returns one of your options with a probability, in one pass. We gave Jev, seven other decision models and the big general models the same 60 fictional candidate reviews, with four questions about each one. The general models, from Gemma 4 26B up to Opus 5.5, cost 3.6 to 37.7 times as much per pass. The best of the other decision models, Solar Decide, matched 55 on its first pass. The cheapest strong result came from a general model: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash with thinking off matched 54 for $0.0027, under half the price of a Jev pass, and 48 when we ran it again. Most of Jev's 6 misses sat on reviews a person would pause on. Two were plain errors, and one of those was a harassment report it marked as needing no follow-up.

If the decision matters, create a rule that hands the hard reviews to a person.

Every review gets four decisions.

The 60 reviews are short, fictional comments from job candidates. A model answers four questions about each one, and a review counts as matched only when all four answers agree with the reference. Every model ran three prompt versions: P0 is the plain task, P1 tells the model it's a classifier, and P2 adds step-by-step decision rules. Each version ran three fresh passes.

  1. How was the experience?Positive, negative, mixed, neutral, or can't tell.
  2. Does someone need to follow up?Yes, no, or can't tell.
  3. Does it report a serious concern?Harassment, discrimination or a privacy breach, for example. A bad experience alone does not count.
  4. Could it work as a testimonial?Yes, no, or can't tell.

Seven decision models, Jev and the best general models, pass by pass.

Each cell counts the reviews where all four answers matched, out of 60. Cost is what one pass over all 60 reviews cost.

A decision model does one job. You send it the text and a question with a fixed list of answers, and it returns one answer with a probability in a single pass, with no reasoning and no free text. That makes it fast and cheap, and it also means it never stops to say the question does not fit. A general model reads the same instructions as a prompt and can reason before it answers, which costs more.

Decision models
Jev 1.13 (TypeSafe), Solar Decide (Upstage), Clef and Clef Flash (Cloudflare), Perplexity Decider 27B (Perplexity), Luna Decisions (OpenAI), Tev 1 4B (Together) and Liquid d1 (Liquid AI) ran every prompt three times. Kev, Laya, OpenJev, SemIf and AnyJev were tested too.
General models
Claude Opus, Sonnet, Haiku and Fable (Anthropic), GPT-6 and GPT-5.6 through Codex (OpenAI), Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini Flash (Google), Gemma 4 from E2B to 31B (Google), Qwen3 from 0.6B to Qwen3.8 27B (Alibaba), DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Mistral Small, hosted and local.

Every configuration, every pass ↗

The gap is five reviews.

Over three fresh passes, Jev matched 54, 53 and 52 of 60. Opus 5.5 at high effort matched 59, 58 and 58. Sonnet 5.5 at xhigh matched 58 in all nine cells. The best decision model on its first pass was Solar Decide, at 55.

A Jev pass over all 60 reviews cost six tenths of a cent. An Opus pass costs 22 cents at API prices.

Jev flagged all 25 reviews the reference marks as a serious concern. It still answered no to "does someone need to follow up?" on this one, where the reference says yes:

Nice staff. After the trial shift the manager kept asking me out even after I said no twice. I've asked the recruiter to stop him contacting me.

The reviews that split the models are the ones a person pauses on.

None of the seven decision models matched the reference on this review: 0 of 7. Among general-model configurations with valid answers, 89 of 113 did.

Six reviews split four or more of the seven decision models, and three of them carry disputed reference labels. Five of Jev's six misses are in that set, so the misses were not random and you can route reviews like these to a person.

A confident answer can still be wrong, and longer prompts made more setups worse.

The confidence score

Every Jev answer comes with a confidence score. The calibration gap is the average distance between how sure a model says it is and how often it is right, so lower is better. Over its 720 answers, Jev's gap was 0.011, against 0.358 for Clef Flash. Jev was still 0.96 confident on a wrong testimonial answer. Its ten least-confident reviews include 7 of the 10 hardest, so the score points at hard reviews, and it still lets a wrong answer through at the top of the scale.

A longer prompt

Going from classifier instructions (P1) to step-by-step decision rules (P2) raised the score for 4 of 39 setups, left 14 unchanged and lowered 21.

What should you do

Four steps, and none of them needs a big budget.

Label 60 of your own cases

Pull 60 of your own cases and label them yourself before any model sees them.

Add can't tell

Give every question a can't-tell option, so a review that doesn't fit has somewhere to go.

Run two cheap models

Run two cheap models and keep only what they agree on.

Count the queue

Count how many land in the human queue, and check that the rare cases you care about got through.

About this test

  • The 60 reviews are fictional. An AI assistant wrote them and drafted the reference answers, and a person checked all 60. Three labels are still disputed: DEV-006, DEV-013 and DEV-030.
  • Each row is one setup: the model plus its route, effort and prompt. Change any of those and the score can move.
  • Every number on this site describes these 60 reviews. Request times include the network and batching, so we make no speed claim.
