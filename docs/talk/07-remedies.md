## 1. Consequences for a team shipping this

Assume the four judgments drive the queues in the labeling guide: escalation review, clarification review, candidate follow-up, testimonial shortlist and analytics.

1. Off-topic input gets a confident answer. A restaurant review, a test message or a complaint meant for another company gets four definite labels. It then lands in analytics, and sometimes in follow-up or the shortlist, but never in clarification. On the soup review every decision model answered "no" on the three business fields at confidence 0.84 to 1.00 (01b section 8). A confidence gate would not have caught it, and nothing in the output says "this is not about hiring".
2. "Can't tell" turns into "no" on the escalation field. Where the reference says "insufficient information" on serious concern, runs answered "no" 25.9% of the time overall and 40.3% of the time in decision-model runs (01b section 2.3). The guide routes every such case to clarification review. A collapse to "no" quietly removes an underspecified allegation from every human queue. For a compliance owner this matters more than the headline score.
3. A well-calibrated model is still confidently wrong on single reviews. Jev's pooled calibration error (ECE) is 0.011. It was still wrong at 0.96 on a testimonial and at 0.91 on the soup review's concern field, and Solar, Liquid and Luna were wrong at 1.00 (01b section 8). "Auto-accept above 0.9" keeps those errors and holds back correct answers: Jev's sentiment at 0.9 withholds 13 reviews, 9 of them right.
4. Every prompt edit is a model change. Going from the framed prompt to the decision-tree prompt made 21 of 39 setups worse and 4 better. Clef fell 54, 51, 49 in every pass, and the shift went in different directions for decision models and general models (01 insight 4, 01b section 12.6). A standard operating procedure pasted into a prompt can move the label distribution with no visible change in the total.
5. The total hides the class the business pays for. Answering "no" to every testimonial scores 50 of 60. Gemma E2B thinking-off found 11 to 12 of 25 serious concerns with zero false alarms while scoring 30 to 34 overall (01b section 6). Marketing loses testimonials and compliance loses escalations, and neither shows up in a four-field total.
6. Vendor choices are being made inside the noise. Three disputed labels move runs by up to 3 points. On 60 items, a score of 54 has a 95% Wilson interval of 79.9% to 95.3%, and 59 has 91.1% to 99.7% (computed here; formula at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_proportion_confidence_interval). On first passes Opus matched five reviews Jev missed and missed none that Jev matched, which gives an exact two-sided McNemar p of 0.0625 (computed here). One pass of 60 does not separate them at the usual 0.05 level. The three-pass repeats (Jev 52 to 54, Opus 58 to 59) are what make the gap believable.
7. A deterministic model is not a correct one, and hosted output is not frozen. 31 of 50 decision groups changed no answer in three passes, so their errors repeat at any volume. General models flip 5 to 9 hard reviews between passes (01b section 12.4). Cloudflare's board, generated 2026-10-01, notes that Jev's answers on its latency sample "differ from the Sep-19 board rows in 20 choices", with scores unchanged and no new version (https://clef-evals.workers-ai-mle.workers.dev/data/leaderboard.json). Validation done once does not certify next month.
8. An invalid answer becomes a silent default. Output failures cluster on the hard reviews, not the long ones (01b section 12.9). A retry-until-valid loop or a default value turns a useful "could not parse" signal into an answer.

## 3. Majority voting, answered precisely

The question was whether running the same classifier dozens, hundreds or thousands of times and taking the majority fixes the errors. Short answer: not for a deterministic decision head, barely for a frontier model on this task, and the literature's large-N gains belong to tasks with a verifier or to reasoning chains. Variety has to come from different models, prompts or reordered options.

### 3.1 What the literature reports

| Source | Setting | N tested | Result |
|-|-|-|-|
| Self-consistency, Wang et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2203.11171) | Math and commonsense reasoning chains | Up to 40 paths | Gains over chain of thought: GSM8K +17.9, AQuA +12.2, ARC-c +3.9 points. UL2-20B on AQuA: 25.3 at 10 paths, 26.7 at 20, 26.9 at 40. The authors say performance "saturates quickly" in most cases. |
| More Agents, Li et al. (https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05120) | Sampling and voting | Up to 40 | GPT-3.5 GSM8K 0.73 to 0.85, MMLU 0.59 to 0.70. Gains grow with task difficulty and taper at the hardest setting. No plateau point reported. |

No source we found recommends an N for label classification. A same-model majority gain for text classification is not cleanly measured anywhere we looked.

### 3.2 What our saved runs say

`s13_majority_vote.py` takes every configuration-condition with three or more passes (252 groups, the same grouping as `s07_repeatability.py`), votes each field 2 of 3, and scores against the reference. It also tests a self-unanimity rule: accept a review only when all three passes return the identical answer.

| Scope | Groups | No answer changed in 3 passes | Majority beat the average pass | Tied | Lost | Beat the best pass | Mean gain, reviews of 60 |
|-|-:|-:|-:|-:|-:|-:|-:|
| Decision, all | 50 | 31 | 15 | 33 | 2 | 7 | +0.89 |
| General, all | 202 | 11 | 89 | 55 | 58 | 14 | +0.15 |
| Decision, every pass 60 valid, mean 54 or more | 5 | 4 | 0 | 5 | 0 | 0 | 0.00 |
| General, every pass 60 valid, mean 54 or more | 145 | 8 | 63 | 43 | 39 | 6 | +0.14 (range -1.3 to +2.0) |

What the decision rows mean. In 13 of the 15 decision groups where the vote beat the average, one pass had failed or invalid answers that the vote covered over. Jev's interrupted P2 pass, with 17 valid answers, is one: 54, 15, 54 votes to 54. OpenJev's generated-JSON passes with format failures are six more. Jev's three-pass majority is 54 in five of its six conditions and 53 in the sixth (OpenRouter P0, passes 54, 53, 52); it never beat Jev's best single pass. Voting repaired dropped answers; it did not change a judgment. The two all-valid decision gains were OpenJev thinking P0 (53, 54, 54 voted to 56) and Solar P1 (53, 52, 51 voted to 53).

What the general rows mean. For strong frontier and open configurations, a three-vote majority added on average a seventh of a review per 60, and lost against the average pass in 39 of 145 groups.

Self-agreement is weak evidence. Across the 145 strong general groups the unanimity rule accepted 8,281 of 8,700 positions with 211 reference errors; only 28 groups reached zero accepted errors. Those errors sit on the disputed reviews: DEV-013 in 70 groups, DEV-030 in 44, DEV-006 in 40, DEV-010 in 20. Among the 5 strong decision groups it accepted 296 of 300 with 27 errors and no group at zero. Compare 423 of 853 cross-model charged pairs at zero accepted errors (01b section 11). Those are different populations, so read it as a direction. Running the same model twice is not a second opinion: Qwen 35B thinking-off agrees with itself on 9 wrong answers, and Jev's original and fresh1 passes agree on 59 reviews with 5 errors (01b section 11, Table A and Table B self-repeat lines).

### 3.3 The answer by N

| N | Same deterministic decision head (Jev, Clef, Perplexity) | Same frontier or open LLM | Different models, prompts or option orders |
|-|-|-|-|
| 1 | The answer you will get every time. | One draw from a spread of 3 to 6 points (01b section 12.4). | Not applicable. |
| 3 to 5 | Repairs failed calls only. Jev: $0.018 for three passes of 60. | Mean +0.14 reviews per 60 here. Use flips as a defer signal, but unanimity still accepts the shared errors. | Two models with identical answers: zero accepted errors in 5 of 21 native pairs and 423 of 853 charged pairs. Option reversal is untested here. |
| 10 to 40 | No change. Jev N=10 is $0.059 per 60. | Reasoning-task gains flatten by 10 to 20 samples (Wang et al.). Opus N=10 is about $2.22 per 60 (API-equivalent estimate). Adaptive stopping cuts the samples needed by up to 7.9x. | AnyJev reads about 10.6 of 18 rotations before its stopping rule fires (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00831). |
| Hundreds to thousands | No change. | Useful only with a verifier that can check an answer, as with code tests. A label has no such check at run time. Without one, voting plateaus (Brown et al.), and on hard items it can get worse (Chen et al.). | Bounded by correlated errors: 60% agreement when both models err (Kim et al.). |

Recommendation: spend the second call on a different model, not a second sample. On this set, two cheap general models at $0.0686 accepted 58 with zero errors on one draw. Three passes of Gemma 26B cost about $0.063 at its observed $0.0211 per pass and bought an average of 0.14 reviews in the comparable groups. For a deterministic head, the cheap source of variety is reordering the options (AnyJev L0), and that needs its own test.

## 4. Fine-tuning

The question: would fine-tuning a small model on a few hundred labelled reviews beat zero-shot decision models and frontier LLMs on this four-field task?

What the evidence says:
1. Fine-tuned encoders beat zero-shot models of the 2023 to 2024 generation on most classification tasks. Bucher and Martini found fine-tuned models at 0.92 accuracy on news sentiment against 0.87 for zero-shot GPT-4, and 0.94 on stance against 0.61 for Claude Opus. Their learning curves "begin to saturate after around 200 labels", with 200 to 500 as the practical range (https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08660v2). Edwards and Camacho-Collados found RoBERTa-large at 0.727 micro-F1 against 0.416 for zero-shot Flan-T5 over 16 datasets. The gap grows with the number of classes (https://arxiv.org/html/2403.17661v2). Neither tested 2026 frontier models or decision models.
2. A handful of labels can be enough for common classes. SetFit claims 8 labelled examples per class are "competitive with fine-tuning RoBERTa Large on the full training set of 3k examples" on one sentiment set (https://huggingface.co/docs/setfit/index). ModernBERT gives 149M and 395M encoders with an 8,192-token context (https://huggingface.co/blog/modernbert).
3. Rare classes are the constraint. In a BERT study, about 25 minority-class examples were needed before learning started, with a plateau near 100; that was entity tagging, not review classification (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.00828). At this set's reference rates, 400 labelled reviews would hold about 60 testimonials and 167 serious concerns, but only 7 to 40 "insufficient" cells per field (computed from the v0.2 distribution). "Insufficient" would stay under-supplied unless deliberately oversampled.
4. One 2026 head-to-head exists, on an easy task. Sebastian Raschka (29 September 2026) found ModernBERT reached about 95% on IMDb after 23 minutes of fine-tuning, and Jev reached 96.47% zero-shot on the 25,000 test reviews in about 22 minutes for $0.65. He notes "we don't know if the IMDb test set was part of the training set". His rule of thumb: fine-tune a custom classifier for a task you will run repeatedly at high volume; use Jev for one-off work (https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/classifier-history-and-jev). A hobby comparison on five tabular datasets reports macro-F1 of ModernBERT 0.69, gpt-6-luna 0.70 and Jev 0.58, with plain QDA at 0.76. The author calls it "not a research quality code base" (https://github.com/cfu288/jev-vs-ml-classifiers). We found no comparison of Jev against a fine-tuned classifier on a multi-field, rubric-defined text task like this one.
5. The followers' own recipes are fine-tunes, and they trail Jev on breadth. Nimble trained a LoRA on 2,676 synthetic examples and scored 90.12% against Jev's 93.21% on its own holdout (https://github.com/bespokelabsai/nimble). Kev-4B's held-out breadth index is 38.0 against Jev's 54.0 (https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev). In our runs, open decision models not trained on these labels scored 39 (Alex OpenJev 4B), 46 to 49 (Kev-4B) and 53 to 54 (OpenJev native) (01 insight 12).
6. Maintenance moves to you. A fine-tuned head has a fixed label set and cannot read a changed rubric at run time, so a rubric change means relabelling and retraining. That follows from the architecture; we did not fetch a concept-drift survey (Gama et al. 2014 returned 403). Closed-set classifiers are also weak on off-topic input (CLINC150, section 2), so fine-tuning does not fix finding 1.

The middle paths, cheapest first:
1. A calibration layer on a decision model's probabilities, fitted on your labels: temperature scaling, per-field cost thresholds. It keeps the rubric at run time and needs a validation set, not a training program.
2. Fine-tune a released decision checkpoint rather than a bare base model. Kev documents `kev.train --init_from jaredpalmer/kev-4b` on a JSONL of labelled questions with a 10% to 20% holdout, and its fine-tune helper estimates about $1 on an H100 (https://github.com/jaredpalmer/kev). Cloudflare offers Clef fine-tuning as a hands-on service and warns you "may give up some general purpose performance in exchange for higher accuracy in a specific domain" (https://blog.cloudflare.com/clef-decision-models/).
3. A fine-tuned encoder as a third vote in agree-or-defer. Kim et al. find errors correlate more between similar and same-provider models (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07962v1). An encoder trained only on your labels plausibly fails differently from two LLMs. That is a hypothesis, not a measured result.

Verdict: fine-tuning is the remedy for rare-class recall and a fixed label set at high volume, once you hold 200 to 500 labels with enough rare positives. It does not fix off-topic input, it does not remove the need for a held-out calibration set, and on this benchmark the 340 records it would need do not exist yet.

## 5. How to make the benchmark better

1. Build a validation and test split. Generate the 340 planned records, lock a test split that is never used to choose prompts or thresholds, and report Wilson intervals. At 400 items a 90% score narrows from 79.9%-95.3% to 86.7%-92.6%.
2. Use independent human annotators. Two people per item, blind to model outputs and to each other, plus an adjudicator. Report Krippendorff's alpha per field, keep disputed items flagged, and publish a v0.3 reference with an adjudication record (the labeling guide already requires that record).
3. Add controls for what broke. About 20 off-topic texts (restaurant, product, IT ticket, an employee review of the same firm, spam), about 20 "insufficient information" items per field, and a handful of embedded-instruction items ("publish this"). Today there is one off-topic review, and four of the ten insufficient cells come from it.
4. Run a calibration study. Keep probabilities for every native answer (the adapters already validate them). Plot per-field reliability and risk-coverage curves on validation, fit thresholds there and report on test. Add conformal sets once n allows.
5. Add variety runs. Option-order reversal for every decision model, three prompt wordings, and the relevance-first variant, with three passes each.
6. Pin and monitor serving. Record model ID, route, endpoint snapshot (`catalog`), batch size and date per request. Run a weekly canary on the frozen 60 and alarm on per-item flips.
7. Complete the cost ledger. Observed charges, estimates and unknown holds are already separate. Add human review minutes per deferred case: it is the unmeasured cost behind every agree-or-defer number.
8. Do the statistics. Paired McNemar tests for model A against model B on the same items, intervals on every score and every rare-class recall, and three passes before any configuration is chosen (01b section 12.8).
9. Pair Jev offline with the seven native models and the general runs. It needs no inference and closes Q&A 13.
